# Session log — 2026-09-07: the ledger of built things

Supplements [`HANDOFF-2026-09-07.md`](HANDOFF-2026-09-07.md), which remains the operational
snapshot. This log records one change: a deterministic, complete ledger of every built mass
in the world beneath the model-curated landmark tier.

## The problem

A player-built thing existed in the present tense only if the vision model chose to draw a
box around it, and the segmentation prompt told it outright to leave out paths, scattered dirt
and pillars. So the model gated *existence*, not just meaning. Measured on the live database
before the change:

| fact | value |
|---|---:|
| block events with no `place_id` | 1,864 of 3,962 (47%) |
| `work_events` tagged `trace` | 30 of 61 |
| present-tense rows for a dirt pillar a player climbed | 0 |

The pillar at `placement_316_71_-294` (18 dirt, 1x11x5) was history only. Asked "who built
that pillar", the god had nothing to point at, and asked "what have I built here" it could not
mention it at all.

## The design: masses beneath landmarks

`agent/masses.py`. One row per connected mass of built blocks in every patch of ground that has
been read, whatever its size and whoever put it there. Code measures it, code attributes it from
exact block history, code matches its identity across reads. No model decides whether a mass
exists. A landmark in `structures` is now a named grouping of masses; each structure records
`comprises`, and each mass records `place_id`.

What a mass row carries, all measured:

- box, block count, dims, fill, complete material census
- `levels`: blocks per height, so "how many floors" is answerable from a profile
- `footing`: how many of its lowest blocks rest on ground, over air, over water, on other
  built blocks; `natural_contact`: blocks pressed against terrain
- `origin`: `player_built` only when the latest recorded mutation at at least half its
  blocks is a placement of the material standing there; `world_generated` inside a known
  generated site; otherwise `unknown`, stated as such
- `builders`: actor to share; `observed_share`: how much the ledger can vouch for
- `first_built_ms`, `last_edited_ms`, `edits` (separate visits ten minutes apart), gross
  `placed` and `broken`
- `partial`: which faces of the read cut it off; `lineage`: what it split from or absorbed

Provenance is SCANNED at full confidence. A separate INFERRED `role:<id>` relationship
(`landmark | utility | decoration | debris | unknown` plus a noun) is asked of the cheap text
model for unnamed masses at or above the eight-block floor, from facts only, and cached by a
hash of those facts. The role never gates the row.

## Rules that had to be decided

**Dirt counts when history says a player placed it.** `is_natural` exists to drop terrain, and
it drops dirt, which is what most pillars and ramps are made of. `placed_naturals()` adds back
any natural-material block whose latest recorded mutation is a player placement of that exact
material. The world stays the authority on what stands; the ledger only says which of it was put
there on purpose. This changes what the segmentation model is shown too: `built` in
`resegment` now includes them.

**A partial view may only grow a record.** A mass within reach of the read edge is
`partial` (within one block, because connectivity reaches two). If it matches, or lies against,
a record known to extend beyond the window, the record becomes the union: the box grows, the
census gains exactly the fragment's blocks outside the old box, a face is marked cut only where
nothing is known past the edge, and the row is flagged `union_of_views`. Only a read that holds
the whole record may shrink it or set it exactly. Overwriting with the fragment lost the far
end; leaving the record untouched lost every extension built past the edge; and one-to-one
matching that gave a record to the bigger of two fragments minted the smaller as a new row on
every rebuild. The god queues a look past each cut face, but only into a region cell a player
has occupied, and not again within five minutes.

**Only measured absence retires.** A row fully inside a fresh read with no built blocks left in
its box is gone. One whose blocks now sit in a differently shaped mass is deleted with an
`absorbed:<new>:<old>` relationship on the survivor. Nothing is retired for being unmentioned,
because nothing in this layer is mentioned by anyone.

**Exact containment beats a radius for history.** `activity.record_activity` resolved a place as
`generated or structure`, and a village site claims a 128-block radius, so a pillar built beside
a village was filed under the village. Now a measured generated building or a workstation clue
keeps the generated place, and a broad site yields to the landmark or mass the event happened
in. `masses.relink()` applies the same rule to old rows with no place at startup and to the
window after every read.

## What the first full rebuild taught

The first read of every occupied cell produced 1,608 masses, 1,420 of them "unknown
builder" with 18k blocks, and six settlement structures anchored to no mass. Three causes,
each fixed in code rather than in the data:

- **Plain connectivity fused the settlement.** Hall, pen, tower and rails, joined by fences
  and paths, were one 632-block mass that no landmark held half of. Masses now never cross
  a landmark box (structures and measured generated buildings, smallest wins) and never
  cross the seam between blocks history credits to a player and blocks it does not. The
  boxes the model drew are the judgement of where one thing ends; the ledger honours them
  and is re-cut after every segmentation pass. A dirt pillar leaned on a village wall stays
  the player's pillar.
- **Terrain the filter had never seen.** Tall seagrass, bushes, sunflowers, lilacs, bee
  nests, amethyst geodes with their smooth basalt shells. Added to the natural list in
  `render.py`; a player who places one is still credited by exact history.
- **Trees.** Logs are deliberately not natural because a log is as often a wall. The ledger
  drops a connected mass made only of logs, credited to nobody, that touches leaves. A log
  cabin touches no leaves; a trunk the player stacked is credited.

Two rebuilds of the unchanged world then had to agree. The first pass at that retired 94
rows and minted twelve new ids: fragments through overlapping window edges, and log columns
cut by the top of a slab that the next slab up recognised as trees. The union rule above and
"an uncredited all-log mass cut by the read edge waits to be seen whole" brought it to zero
retirements and zero id changes between consecutive rebuilds.

The ledger now holds 295 masses: 60 player-built, 21 inside known generated sites, 214
unknown. The unknowns are real: three abandoned mineshafts, two dungeons, a trial chamber
wall, an outpost-like building. The seed environment service does not locate those structure
types, so they cannot be called generated on evidence. Extending it is follow-up work.

A generated fallback that anchored sixty blocks of village paths to one house was corrected
to anchor to the village site: a component building claims a mass only by containing it.

## Loop cadence and the cost of looking

Watching the elephant get found showed three things wrong with how the loop spends its
looks. Each is a rule now, with a test.

- **Structure detection settles in ten seconds, not forty-five.** `RESEGMENT_SETTLE_TICKS`
  in `god.py` is separate from the quest debounce, which keeps its own timing. The sweep
  runs every five seconds while anything is pending.
- **A follow-up past a read edge measures; it does not ask.** Every "queued a look beyond
  it" was a full pass with two vision calls, on ground the model had already judged. A
  follow-up is now ledger-only. It earns a real look only by revealing a whole player-built
  mass of eight blocks or more that no landmark holds: something new, or something only
  ever seen cut before.
- **An unchanged ledger is not boxed twice.** After every pass the god remembers the exact
  set of masses under that cell; if a later disturbance settles and the ledger is identical,
  the vision pass is skipped. Settled cells within eight blocks of the one being looked at
  ride along in the same read instead of each buying their own.
- **Renders run off the event loop.** The software renderer took fifteen seconds on a full
  patch and ran synchronously, so chat waited behind every look whether or not the question
  had anything to do with it. Every render in the loop now runs in a worker thread.

## When a look is spent

A block placed or broken never causes a vision call by itself. The chain is: the cell is
marked disturbed; after ten quiet seconds it is read and the ledger updated, which is code
only; then `God._look_decision` decides whether the model is asked to draw boxes. A look is
spent in exactly these cases:

| release | rule |
|---|---|
| unchanged | the masses under the cell are identical to the last boxing there: never |
| first look | a whole player-built mass of eight or more blocks that no landmark holds and no pass has been shown (`looked_at_ms` on the mass): once, immediately if the builder has left the window, otherwise after sixty seconds |
| threshold | at least `MCGOD_LOOK_THRESHOLD` block changes (default 32) in the cells under the window since they were last boxed |
| reference | a chat question names a structure inside a deferred window, or is asked from inside one: the look is paid before the answer, at most two a question |
| idle | no block placed anywhere for ten minutes: deferred looks are paid oldest first |

Anything else is deferred: the cell sits in `edited` with its reason, its structures stay
marked contradicted, and the ledger keeps their size exact. Context reports a landmark's
size from the masses it comprises when that differs from its own row, so only the name and
description can lag. Demolition is never deferred, because retirement is measured from the
ledger, not from the boxing pass. Contradicted structures inside a deferred window are left
to the deferral rather than re-segmented on their own timer, which would have bypassed the
threshold.

Presence for the first-look wait comes from the movement samples already flowing, kept per
actor in memory; a sample older than thirty seconds no longer says where anyone is.

## What the elephant's gold eyes taught

Two gold blocks set into the cobblestone elephant, four block events, well under the
threshold. The god then described it as 48 cobblestone and 2 buttons, said `NOT HISTORY`
aloud, and read its box out as "301-308 by -303 to -297". Three fixes:

- **Landmark rows carry the ledger's census.** `masses.refresh_landmarks` runs on every
  read: a structure's materials and block count become the sum of the masses it comprises,
  and a structure an edit had marked out of date is reaffirmed (`Store.reaffirm`, the mirror

  only the name; the contents are always current. Demolition also no longer waits for a
  look: `God._retire_gone` applies the boxing pass's fifteen-percent rule on every read.
- **The history sentinel is matched loosely.** The model answered `NOT HISTORY` with a
  space; the exact comparison missed it and the reply was spoken. Any spelling of the
  sentinel before evidence has been retrieved now means "not historical".
- **A box read out as ranges is a coordinate.** The gate stripped triples only. Two number
  ranges joined by "by" are a box and are removed; "10-17 blocks away" is a distance and
  stays.

The history prompt also now says to look a named thing up in `relationships` and the
structure tables before concluding it is unrecorded, and that a build's materials are
re-measured on every read.

## The seed map: the world nobody has visited

Asked how many villages lie within ten thousand blocks, the god said eight. It had counted rows
in `generated_features` with `kind LIKE '%village%'`: five sites it happened to have located for
earlier questions, plus three component buildings of one of those. The true answer is 272.

The immediate fix was to tell the evidence model that a row with a parent is a building, not a
place, and that those rows are only what has been found so far. But the real gap was that
nothing could count at all. Minecraft's structure placement is a grid of regions with one
candidate each, drawn from the seed, the region coordinates and a per-structure salt; the biome
generator decides which candidates survive. Both are pure functions of the seed, so the whole
world is computable without visiting any of it.

`agent/seedmap.py` does that, over vendored cubiomes in `agent/cubiomes/` (MIT). Upstream stops
at 1.21; carrying it to 26.2 is described in `cubiomes/PROVENANCE.md` and took two artefacts
from the game itself: the biome parameter list the server's own data generator emits, and a port
of `Climate$RTree` read out of the server jar with `javap`. Measured against the running
server's sampler, 46,315 points across all three dimensions match with zero mismatches, and
every structure position checked matches the server's own locate.

Three properties make it safe to let a model call:

- **It says what it is.** Every result carries `as_generated`, because a god that forgets this
  will describe a village the player burned down.
- **It says how sure it is.** Placement is exact; the terrain check is exact for most structure
  types and misses for a few, measured per type against the server and recorded in
  `seedmap.ACCURACY`. An approximate result carries its miss rate and asks to be confirmed.
- **It never writes.** Persisting hundreds of computed sites would drown the record of places
  someone has actually been, which is what `generated_features` is for.

Wiring it required widening the evidence loop. `answer_if_history` classified "how many villages
are within 10k" as NOT_HISTORY, correctly, and returned before any tool ran. The loop is the
project's one model-directed evidence path, so it now answers world questions too: a historical
claim must still retrieve `event_history`, and a world claim must retrieve seed or environment
evidence. One further correction after the first live run: a count that stopped at the listing
cap reported "at least 200" for a number it could have known exactly, so only `nearest` may stop
early.

Live, through the real pipeline:

- "how many villages are there within 10k blocks of me" -> "There are 272 villages within 10,000
  blocks of you, according to the world's seed generation."
- "where is the nearest woodland mansion" -> "about 3,580 blocks away, in a dark forest to the
  southeast", which Paper independently confirms.
- "what biome is 20000 blocks straight north of me" -> "snowy plains".

`seedmap_truth.json` pins known answers, several of them independently confirmed by Paper's own
locate, so a version bump or a library change fails the suite rather than quietly describing a
different world.

### Biomes, and the reach the god thought it had

Asked whether ice spikes were nearby, the god said no; asked where the closest one was, it said
"I couldn't find an ice spikes biome within 4,096 blocks". That number is the plugin RPC's
limit, not the world's, and the seed map had no biome-search mode at all. It now has two:

- **nearest_biome** searches outward in rings from a point. The nearest ice spikes on this
  world is 6,233 blocks south, and the server confirms that exact block is ice spikes.
- **biome_distribution** counts biomes over a radius. Asked for the most common biome within
  1,000 blocks the god had said plains; ocean covers 32% and plains 10%, and the plugin's own
  sampling agrees to a tenth of a percent.

Not finding something is reported as not finding it *here, at this sampling step*, never as the
thing not existing: a patch smaller than the step can be stepped over. The tool description now
states outright that its reach exceeds the server-backed tool's, so "nothing within 4,096
blocks" is a reason to ask the seed map rather than an answer.

### The nearest village to someone standing in a village

Asked for the closest village other than the one they were in, the god named the one they were
standing in, 43 blocks away. The computation was right: from that spot the nearest village
attempt is the player's own village, and the next real one is 1,076 blocks off. What the model
lacked was any way to tell that this particular coordinate was its own village, because a
seed-map position is only a number and the identity lives in the store.

So code supplies the link. Every seed-map result that coincides with a generated site already on
record now carries that site's id, kind and name, and the site the player currently occupies,
from `actor_presence`, is marked `you_are_here`. Component buildings never match, so a church
inside a village cannot masquerade as a second village. What to do with the mark stays the
model's decision, and the prompt says the obvious thing: when they ask for a different one, skip
it. The answer became "a taiga village about 1,076 blocks away, to the northeast".

### Coordinates, in every notation a model reaches for

The first live run leaked "around [-1332, 5716]" and then "around X -1,332, Z 5,716". The gate
only matched triples, and a horizontal position names two axes, which is exactly how somewhere
far away gets reported. It now catches bracketed pairs and axis-labelled forms while leaving
distances and large numbers alone, and the history prompt says to describe places by direction
and distance in the first place.

The model also wrote "near the generated location [coordinates omitted]". Announcing the
omission is the same failure as making it: it tells the player about the rules instead of about
the world. That placeholder is removed with the other policy announcements, and unconditionally,
since it appears precisely when no coordinate is present to trigger the coordinate gate.

## Ten minutes for a sentence

A player asked what had just happened to them and waited several minutes. Timing every call
in the evidence loop found it at once: a single ordinary model call took **609 seconds**.

Everything else was fast. One call is one to nine seconds with tools, with an image, with two
hundred kilobytes of evidence, or all three at once; the same request repeated came back in a
second. So it was not the model, the prompt, the payload or the reasoning setting. It was the
provider, on that one request, and nothing in prompt or model choice prevents that recurring.

The defect was that **nothing timed a model call out**. A stalled request is not a slow answer,
it is a silent god, which from the player's side is indistinguishable from the thing being
down. Two bounds now:

- **Per call**, forty-five seconds and one more attempt. Providers route each request
  separately, so the retry usually lands somewhere else and returns in a second.
- **Per answer**, a forty-second gathering budget. Per-call deadlines bound one request; six
  tool rounds of them still add to minutes. Past the budget the tools are withheld, which is
  what forces an answer rather than another query. Running out of time never becomes a way to
  answer from nothing: without retrieved evidence it still refuses.

Measured after: 4 to 18 seconds for the same questions, against 621 before.

## One broken shrub, three minutes of scanning

Pulling up a single shrub set the god reading the world for over three minutes, thirty-odd
scans walking across a whole village. Two mechanisms compounded, and a third wasted model
calls on top.

- **Vegetation counted as a block change.** Any place or break marked its cell for a fresh
  look, and picking a flower is a break. Nothing in a mass can change that way, so a break
  of a natural block is now ignored unless it lies inside a mass on record. That last clause
  matters: a dirt pillar is made of dirt, and taking one down must still be noticed.
- **Follow-ups followed themselves across untouched ground.** A village's paths are one
  connected mass hundreds of blocks long, so every read cut it, queued a look beyond, and
  that look cut it again somewhere new.

  Stopping the chain outright was the wrong fix and was corrected: a build can be longer
  than one read, and following on is exactly how a long bridge gets measured whole. The
  chain must continue; what it must not do is continue across ground nobody touched. So the
  test is the player's own work. A read with no unspent block changes under it has nothing
  to complete and does not follow, which means a chain runs the length of what someone built
  and stops where their work does. Three further bounds: only a mass worth completing is
  followed, meaning the player's own work or something small enough to be one object; at
  most three edges per read; and a depth cap of six as a backstop, so no arrangement of
  changed ground can walk the scan forever.
- **Roles were re-asked for a block.** The role prompt is content-hashed, so a path growing
  by one block produced a new hash and another call. One mass was asked four times in three
  minutes and flapped between "landmark" and "utility" for the same dirt path. A role is now
  re-asked only when the mass changed by a quarter or more: what a thing is for does not
  change when it grows by a block.

While pinning this, the startup idempotence test turned out to be comparing two different
worlds whenever a server was running, because it replayed a session file that was still being
written. `sync_world` now takes an explicit session list so the test replays fixed evidence.

## The god could see where you were and was not allowed to say

Asked what structure they were standing in, inside a generated village house, the god
rendered the place, described it correctly, and then refused its own answer: "I cannot ground
that answer reliably enough to give it."

Grounding resolved references only against `structures`, which holds player-built objects. A
generated building was outside its vocabulary entirely, so naming one counted as introducing
an ungrounded place and the guard threw the answer away. The guard was right about its own
rule; the rule had a hole.

Three changes, all deterministic:

- **Where you are standing is grounded.** The innermost measured box containing the player,
  from either ontology, is supplied as a fact with the role "the player is standing in this".
  Smallest box wins, so a house inside a village is the house.
- **Generated places joined the vocabulary.** `resolve_references` searches both tables, so
  "the church" resolves, and — the part that actually closed the loop — the guard can match
  the answer's own words back to the fact that grounded them.
- **Presence is reported, and kept separate.** A village site has an anchor and no bounds, so
  being in one is a radius rather than a box. It is rendered as observed presence, never as
  containment. Conflating the two is how "the closest village" once became the one underfoot.

Every item now also carries its origin, which is the difference between "your house" and "a
village house you are standing in".

Before: "I cannot ground that answer reliably enough to give it." After: "You stand in what I
take to be the cobblestone church tower", and from open ground inside the village, "You are
within the plains village, among several recorded works."

## Speech waiting in line

The answer to "what have I built within 100 blocks" was right and took minutes. Timing the
evidence loop in isolation gave 14 seconds, and every suspect measured fast: indexing an event
is 0.6ms, a whole ledger settle including the voxel read is under half a second, a model call
is single digits.

The screenshot had the answer in it. A quest had just completed, and quest judgment is renders
and model calls. Chat was consumed from the same queue by the same single worker as every
other event, so a question sat behind whatever the loop was already doing: a quest being
judged, a build being segmented, any handler that ran long. Each is seconds to a minute of
awaited work, and none of it is the question.

Speech now has its own queue and worker. It is routed before anything can be discarded, so a
question is never dropped to make room the way a heartbeat sample may be. Both workers index
through the same store on the same thread, so nothing races; they simply no longer wait on
each other.

Two renderer calls were also still running on the event loop, the evidence render the chat
itself may request and the terrain view taken at quest time. Both are seconds of pure CPU, and
one of them was blocking the very answer that asked for it. A test now walks the live modules
and fails on any renderer call that is not handed to a thread.

## Counted twice

Asked for everything built within a hundred blocks, the god listed some things more than
once: a gold monument and a gold-block mosaic and several gold markers that were partly the
same gold. It had read the named structures, then the masses beneath them, then reconstructed
further objects from raw block placements, with no rule saying those describe one world.

The data was already right. A mass belonging to a landmark carries that landmark's id, and
`anchor()` keeps `comprises` and `place_id` in agreement, so the masses of an area partition
exactly into those claimed by a structure and those claimed by none. What was missing was the
retrieval rule.

The present-tense answer to "what have I built here" is the structures in that area plus the
masses there whose `place_id` is null. Those two sets are disjoint and together they are
everything that stands. Block placements are history: they include what was later torn down,
and every object they suggest is already one of the rows above, so reading both names the same
thing twice. Two smaller rules went in beside it, both visible in the same answer: a radius is
a circle rather than the square box the SQL selected, and a thing is named by its stored name
or its role noun rather than by its materials.

Measured on the same question from two positions: seven objects and eight objects, each named
once, matching the store exactly. Before, fifteen.

## Robbing yourself

Asked how many items they had stolen, the god answered 491 across 28 withdrawals. It had
counted every container it ever saw the player empty, and 417 of those items came out of
their own chests. The true answer is 74, from three chests they did not place.

The evidence was already there. A `container_take` carries the CONTAINER's coordinates, not
the player's, so a container is theirs exactly when a `block_place` by the same actor sits at
those coordinates. Told that, the model wrote the right query on the first try — and still
answered 491.

Its query compared the stored `minecraft:chest` against the bare word `chest`. That matches
nothing, so the set of their own chests came out empty and every container was classified as
somebody else's. **An empty set is not an error**, which is what made the answer wrong rather
than broken, and this is the same namespace trap that once reported a standing house as 0%
survived. It is now named in the retrieval rules alongside block states, because any
comparison against a material can fall into it.

After both rules: "You stole 74 items in total, taken from three generated chests", and "you
have taken 491 items from chests in total" when the question is collecting rather than theft.

## Mobs killing mobs

The creeper that exploded beside the player also killed a sheep, and nothing recorded it. Only
kills *by* a player were captured, so the blast was on the record and what it cost was not.

`mob_death` is the new event, and the interesting decision was what not to capture. Hostile
mobs burn by the dozen at every sunrise and drown and fall constantly; recording all of it
would be capturing weather. So a death is kept when something else killed it, which is the case
being fixed and is bounded by how often mobs actually fight, and otherwise only when the victim
was not a monster, because a sheep that burned or a wolf that fell is a thing that happened to
the player's world while a zombie catching fire is merely morning.

It carries the victim, its custom name if it had one, whether it was tamed, the cause, and the
killer — followed back through a projectile, because an arrow did not kill the sheep, a
skeleton did. Its actor is `world`, so it is never counted among the player's deeds, and the
evidence prompt says to look for one at the same time and place as an explosion.

## The first action that changes the world

Until now the god could only speak, and CLAUDE.md said plainly that there is no raw command
execution and never will be. That rule is amended deliberately, at the owner's request, and
the reasoning behind it is kept rather than discarded: the agent drafting a command is a
language model, so **what may run is decided in the server**, exactly as the speech budget is.

`CommandService` in the plugin is the gate, and it assumes every command is a mistake until
it matches something safe:

- **An allowlist, never a denylist.** A denylist is a bet that you thought of everything, and
  every release adds commands. Twenty-nine are permitted: giving, summoning, effects, sound,
  light, weather, time, teleporting, building.
- **A forbidden list as well**, redundant by design, so that a future hand widening the
  allowlist carelessly still cannot reach op, stop, reload, ban or whitelist.
- **Nothing that speaks.** say, tell, tellraw and me are refused, because speech is budgeted
  and capped and a command that prints to chat is a way around that budget.
- **`execute` is refused outright**, since it wraps any other command and would make the
  allowlist decorative.
- **Coordinates must be absolute.** A console command's `~` is relative to the world origin,
  not to the player, so a relative build lands somewhere nobody asked for. The model is told
  this in as many words, because otherwise it writes `~` and is refused forever.
- **A fill is measured before the world changes**, at 8192 blocks; the server would refuse a
  huge one eventually, but after the fact and with a message nobody sees.
- **Its own budget**, forty an hour with a half-second cooldown, kept apart from speech: a god
  that talks too much is tiresome, and rationing the two together would let an afternoon of
  conversation quietly spend the ability to act.

The agent holds the same lists in `commands.py`, but only so a refusal is instant and well
worded. It can permit nothing. A mirror that drifts would be worse than none, since it would
explain a rule the server does not have, so a test parses the Java and fails if they disagree.

Two smaller decisions worth keeping. **Acting happens before speaking**: a god that announces
a gift and is then refused has told the player something untrue, and there is no taking it
back, so a failure is admitted in the same breath. And the command's **own output** is
captured through a Paper command sender and returned, because "Gave 5 diamonds" and "No
player was found" are different worlds and reporting success from a dispatch flag is how a
god claims to have given something it did not.

The god's acts land in `event_history` as `god_command` so it can read back what it did. That
needed one general repair: an actor is a player, `world`, or now `god`, and only the first has
a profile or a place. Those were three scattered string comparisons and are now one shared
set, or a command would have invented a player called god and sent the sweep to the origin.

### What the first live command taught

The diamonds arrived and the reply said "I cannot ground that answer reliably enough to give
it". Three faults, each worse than the last.

**A refusal must never hide a gift.** The grounding guard rejects prose that makes unfounded
claims about places. It has no view on whether the player may have what they asked for, and
the action ran regardless — so the player was left holding diamonds beside a sentence denying
one. When an act succeeded and the prose was thrown away, the reply is now built from the
server's own words: "It is done. Gave 10 Diamond to playerone." Never invented; if the
server said nothing, it says only that it is done.

**The guard was firing on prose coincidences.** "I do not grant authority over this world"
names no place, but "world" and "remains" occur in stored descriptions, so every nearby build
resolved and the answer was refused. Reference resolution now records HOW each match was made:
a name is a reference, a word appearing in a stored description is a coincidence. Only a named
place counts as a claim. That was a regression from widening the vocabulary to generated
places, and it also cost a wasted rewrite call on every single reply, so replies got faster
too: "op me" went from a refusal in 8s to a proper answer in 5s.

**A cooldown between commands refused half a request.** "Make it night and give me a pickaxe"
is one request; half a second between commands refused the second half. What needs rationing
is how often the god acts at all, which the hourly budget does, and how much it may do in one
breath, which the agent caps at four.

## Omnipotence, and the two things that are not limits

The caps came off. There is no maximum number of commands in a request, no cooldown between
them, and no hourly budget. "Rain fire around me for a minute" is a command every five ticks
for twelve hundred ticks, and "build a bridge across this chasm" is thousands of blocks;
refusing either would be refusing the point. The fill cap is the game's own 32768 rather than
a smaller one invented here.

Two things remain, and neither restricts what may be asked for.

**The tick.** Bukkit runs commands on the main thread, and a thousand in one tick is a visible
freeze. `CommandRunner` queues everything and drains 200 per tick, so four thousand commands a
second land without the server stuttering. The request is unbounded; the pace is not, and those
are different questions. This is non-negotiable rule 1 and predates the feature.

**A switch.** `/mcgod stop` cancels every running effect and everything still queued. The price
of a god that can do anything is a way to turn it off that does not depend on the agent being
reachable, so it lives in the plugin.

Two capabilities had to be added before "anything" was true:

- **An anchor.** Almost every request is relative to where the player stands — fire above them,
  ice beneath them, a bridge from here — and a console command's `~` is the world origin
  instead. Naming an anchor makes the plugin wrap the command in `execute as X at X run`, so
  `~ ~10 ~` means ten blocks above their head. The wrapping is composed by the server, never
  supplied by the model, which is why `~` became usable without `execute` becoming available.
- **Lasting effects.** "For five minutes" is not a burst; it is a repetition that has to
  survive the agent being busy, restarting, or losing its socket. A spell is commands plus an
  interval plus a duration, scheduled in the plugin, following the anchor, cancelled cleanly
  when the plugin unloads.

Asked for the two examples, the model drafted them correctly on the first try:

| request | drafted |
|---|---|
| fire charges raining around me for a minute | `summon small_fireball ~ ~10 ~ {Motion:[0,-0.5,0]}`, anchored, every 5 ticks for 1200 |
| ice under my feet for five minutes | `setblock ~ ~-1 ~ ice`, anchored, every 2 ticks for 6000 |

What is still refused is only what would take the world away from the people in it: granting
permission, removing a player, changing the server's settings, or printing to chat. That list
is unchanged and is not about creativity.

### Asking for a thing is not asking about the past

"Please give me a netherite sword with max enchants" was answered "I haven't given you a
max-enchanted netherite sword." The evidence loop had claimed the message, read it as "have
you ever given me one", searched the ledger, and truthfully reported that it had not.

Every non-greeting message goes to that loop first, and it decides whether the question is
its own. It was told to step aside for a request, but a request phrased as a courtesy is easy
to read as a question about the past, so the instruction is now emphatic: anything asking you
to DO something is never historical, however it could be rephrased, and a player asking for a
thing wants the thing rather than an account of whether they already have one. Step aside
without looking anything up.

That correction immediately over-swung and had to be pulled back: "what did I build here" also
began stepping aside. What someone has built or done is historical however local it is, and the
prompt now says so with examples.

A second bug surfaced in the same test. The model looked something up, decided the question was
not for it after all, and said the sentinel — but the sentinel was only checked when nothing
had been retrieved, so `NOT_HISTORY` would have been spoken to the player verbatim. It is now
recognised whenever it appears.

Measured after: a request steps aside in one second and the sword arrives in seven, "what did I
build here" is still answered from the ledger, "what am I standing in" still goes to grounding,
and "how many villages within 10k" still goes to the seed map.

### Saying what was accepted is not saying what happened

"So be it. I am giving you a diamond sword bearing the strongest compatible enchantments" —
and no sword arrived. The command had been accepted, queued, and then failed on an item
component the model had spelled the way an older version spelled it.

That was a consequence of pacing. The plugin replied as soon as work was queued, which is
right for a five-minute effect and wrong for a single give: the runner drains two hundred a
tick, so even a large build finishes in well under a second and there is nothing to gain by
answering early. A one-shot batch now answers when it has actually run, with each command's
own result. A spell still answers at once, because nobody waits five minutes for a reply about
a five-minute effect.

Three consequences, all of which the god now gets right:

- **Success is counted from outcomes**, not from acceptance.
- **A claim is withdrawn when nothing worked.** The draft was written expecting the act to
  succeed; appending a correction to a sentence that already claims the gift tells the player
  two different things, so the claim goes instead.
- **A failure is corrected once.** The server says exactly what was wrong, so that goes back
  to the model with the original request and one more attempt is made. Measured: asked for a
  fully enchanted sword, the first draft used `enchantments={levels:{...}}`, the server
  rejected it, and the corrected attempt was a `give` followed by seven `enchant` commands,
  which worked. Once, not a loop — a god that keeps rewriting a command it cannot get right
  is worse than one that admits it.

## Acting, looking, and putting it right

There was already a tool loop in chat, but it could only look: rendering and entity
inspection were tools while commands ran after the loop closed. So the god could see before
it acted and never after. It laid ice for thirty seconds, was asked to clear the mess, ran a
fill that reached nowhere near it, and said the ice was gone.

Acting is now a tool inside that loop. The god can render the area, act, render again, see it
did not work, and try differently before it says anything — and the same loop serves any task,
because looking and acting are just two of the tools it has. The one blind retry built the day
before is gone: the model sees the server's own error itself and corrects in the next round,
which is both simpler and better.

Two things had to change before the loop was any use.

**A command can succeed and change nothing.** A fill whose region held none of the block it
replaces reports "Changed 0 blocks" and calls it success, and the model read that as done.
Code now marks those: they never count as done, and the tool result names them under
`changed_nothing` with the reason. Measured on the ice: before, one fill that reached nothing
and "the mess is gone from around you"; after, the god noticed, widened to the next ring out,
and reported honestly which directions held ice and which did not.

**Every act is recorded where it happened.** "Break the ice I just made" is unanswerable if
the god's own log says only `setblock ~ ~-1 ~ ice` a hundred times: the text is identical each
time and the ice is wherever the player was standing. The runner now records the anchor's
position and dimension, so the god's acts are findable in the same way everything else is.

**And the loop needed a clock.** Eight rounds against a slow provider is minutes; the first
run of the corrected loop took two hundred seconds. It now has the same wall-clock budget the
evidence loop has, sixty seconds, after which the tools are withheld and it must answer from
what it has already done and seen. The per-call deadline came down from forty-five seconds to
twenty-five at the same time, since typical is one to nine and a stall was being paid for
twice.

## Powers, which are not commands

Asked to be made a superhero who could fly and throw fire, the god granted creative mode and
a stack of fire charges. That was the honest limit of what it had: commands were its only
lever, and no command expresses "you can fly but still take damage" or "your empty hand
throws fire".

It does not need a mod. Flight without creative, a fireball from an empty hand, immunity to
the fire you make are all ordinary server-side API and none of them is a command, which is
why the answer was a second way to act rather than a longer allowlist. `PowerService` holds
eighteen: flight, fireball, great_fireball, wind_blast, fire and fall immunity, six potion
effects, glow, and three particle trails. They combine — flight plus fireball plus
fire_immunity plus flame_trail is a flying fire-throwing figure — and they expire, and what
they give back is what was there before, so a granted minute running out never strands a
builder in creative mode mid-air.

Two details that decide whether it feels like a power. Flight is granted **without** creative
mode, so hunger, damage and inventory all still matter; creative is not a superpower, it is a
different game. And a projectile is thrown by right-clicking with an **empty hand**, since a
power that stole block placement and eating would make the game worse rather than better.

## A builder, because shape is a different skill

Asked for a humanoid statue, the dialogue model produced something nobody would recognise as
one. That is not a plumbing failure and no prompt fixes it: holding a shape in mind and
writing the blocks that make it is a different skill from answering a question well.

`design_build` asks a separate model, configured by `MCGOD_BUILD_MODEL`, now
`openai/gpt-6-astra`. The dialogue model decides what to build and where; the builder decides
what blocks that is; the acting loop runs them and looks at the result. Each can be swapped
and measured without touching the others, exactly as segmentation and vision already are.

Measured on a twelve-block hero facing south in stone and iron: 47 commands in 17 seconds,
clearing the space first, laying a base, then placing 39 blocks individually rather than
filling boxes. The acting budget went up to 150 seconds to leave room for design, build and a
look at what was made.

## Clustering

`segment.clusters()` scans every remaining block per frontier step, which is quadratic. On the
settlement cell, `masses.components()` does the same connectivity with neighbour lookups
against a set and measured 1,265 built blocks into 41 masses in under a tenth of a second.
`segment.clusters()` is untouched; the CLI still uses it.

## Guarantees, stated plainly

Where the facts were observed, every question about a built thing is answerable
deterministically: where, how big, what of, who placed what share, when first and last touched,
how many separate visits. Three limits remain and are reported rather than papered over:

1. A thing enters the ledger only when its ground is read, which is after a disturbance
   settles or during a rebuild. Between placement and read, only `event_history` knows.
2. Authorship is unknown for anything built before the tap, in an unloaded session, or in a
   downloaded world. `origin: unknown` and `observed_share` say so.
3. A mass that merges with its neighbour or splits changes id. Lineage is recorded so the
   history stays reachable, but "the same thing" is a geometric judgement.

## Cleaning the existing database

`masses.py --rebuild` reads every occupied region cell in windows sized to the plugin's caps (96
wide, 48 tall, eight-block margins, overlapping slabs), applies the ledger to each, then anchors
and relinks. The eleven curated structures were kept, not re-derived, because the eval showed
cold segmentation merges a settlement into one box. `masses.py --audit` and
`render_structures.py --check` report any structure anchored to no mass, any dangling place,
any duplicate mass, and any history row pointing at a mass that no longer exists.
